01

An oil war that is now being paid for with borrowed money

How a pipeline attack in Saudi Arabia ended up in bond prices, the yen and the cost of AI data centres.

The biggest change in the conversation is where the pain of the Iran war now shows up. For months it was a story about oil. Now it is a story about interest rates and debt. Oil has stayed above $100 a barrel for more than 10 days, and the thing that hurts most is diesel, the fuel that runs trucks, trains and farm machines. In the US, diesel set a new all-time record above $6 a gallon, and it is trading as if Brent crude (the main world oil price) were $180, not the roughly $104 it actually is. So the shortage is in refined fuel, not only in crude oil.

The supply side is getting worse, not better. Saudi Aramco's East-West pipeline, which carries oil across Saudi Arabia to the Red Sea so it can avoid the Strait of Hormuz (the narrow sea channel near Iran through which about a fifth of the world's oil normally passes), was shut after drone strikes on 10 September damaged its pump stations. Saudi Arabia then told European refiners they will get no crude next month. JPMorgan said it can no longer predict where oil goes: it had expected the US government to stop oil crossing $100, petrol crossing $5 a gallon and Treasury yields crossing 5%, and all 3 limits have been broken.

Central banks are reacting to the price rise by raising rates, not cutting them. The US Federal Reserve raised its rate by 0.25% to 3.75%-4% on 16 September, its first rise since 2023. The Bank of Japan raised to 1.25%, the highest in 31 years. At the same time governments and AI companies are borrowing more than ever, so both are competing for the same pool of savings, which is why long-term bond yields keep climbing. Section 03 has the numbers.

For India this cuts both ways. India buys most of its oil, so dear oil hurts. But Indian refiners that export diesel and jet fuel can gain when Europe runs short, and Indian engineering firms may win work rebuilding Gulf oil and gas sites. Section 04 goes through who collects and who pays.

02

The limits that money alone cannot fix

Refineries, power plants, gas tanks and local councils: the physical choke points behind the headlines.

Refining is the first limit. ExxonMobil's Joliet refinery near Chicago, one of the biggest diesel makers in the US Midwest, suffered an unplanned outage, and the region is bracing for a diesel shortage. A refinery cannot be built in a year, so when one goes down the gap is filled only by shipping fuel in from elsewhere, and that is exactly what Europe is also trying to do. US Energy Secretary Chris Wright has suggested drawing down the Strategic Petroleum Reserve (the emergency oil stock the US keeps for a crisis) even faster. The reserve is already very thin, so faster use only brings forward the day it hits its lowest safe level.

Gas is the second limit. Germany's gas storage is at a record low for the season, and the government is looking at paying companies to fill it. Germany has also agreed a cap on fuel prices, which means higher government spending and bigger deficits. Pakistan is running a mini lockdown to save fuel.

India has its own version of the problem, in power. Most integrated steel plants in Raipur have stopped making billets (the rough steel bars that are rolled into rods) and are selling their captive electricity to the grid instead, because power is short and the government is signing supply deals for the next 2 months. Godavari Power & Ispat is selling more than 50% of its power. Less billet supply should lift billet and TMT bar prices, which helps big steel makers and firms like Shyam Metalics, Gallant Ispat and Sambhv Steel.

Why not burn more gas for power? India already tried. It built more than 25 gigawatts of gas-fired plants (1 gigawatt is roughly the output of 1 large nuclear reactor). In 2023-24 that fleet ran at only 14.9% of its capacity, 31 plants with nearly 8 gigawatts made no power at all in FY25, and 5.3 gigawatts was retired in April 2025. The reason is money: India has little gas of its own, so it buys LNG (gas cooled into liquid and shipped) in dollars and sells the power in rupees to state power companies that pay late. About 17 gigawatts is being looked at for restart, but a restart creates repair work, not orders for new turbines, so it is a small business, not a gas boom.

AI has a physical limit too. More than 300 local governments in the US have moved to pause data centres, and analysts say there is now an oversupply of early-stage projects that will never get built. In response the Trump administration is reviewing data centres on 17,600 acres of public land in Arizona, Idaho, Nevada, Oregon, Utah and Wyoming. If Anthropic and OpenAI each go from about 5 to 10 gigawatts by the end of 2027, the 2 labs alone need about 10 gigawatts of new capacity that year, which is 33%-50% of all the capacity normally added in a year. So power, not chips, decides how fast AI revenue can grow.

03

Money and rates: everyone wants to borrow at once

Rate rises, record debt, record leverage and a yen that fell even after Japan raised rates.

The Bank of Japan raised its rate from 1% to 1.25% on 18 September, but the yen fell past ¥157 to the dollar anyway. The reason is the vote: it was 7 to 2, and the 2 members who voted against wanted no rise at all. Traders read that as a sign that further rises will be slow, so they sold the yen, and the central bank then made a "rate check" call to banks to scare sellers. This matters because many investors borrow cheaply in yen to buy assets elsewhere (the "yen carry trade"). A fast rise in the yen would force them to sell those assets, so a weak yen is keeping that risk asleep for now.

Europe's bond market shows the strain. France's 10-year government bond yield rose 11 basis points (0.11%) to 4.56%, the highest since 2008, and the gap between French and German 10-year yields reached 1% for the first time since 2012. A wider gap means lenders see France as riskier. In the US, the 10-year yield touched 4.9981%, just under 5%.

Put together, the US government, AI companies and leveraged hedge funds are all leaning on the same bond market at the same time, while the banks that normally soak up new bonds have little room left. That is why long yields rise even when the economy looks soft: US industrial production was flat in August against an expected 0.3% rise, and factory output fell 0.3%. President Trump wants rates at 1% or lower, and the interest bill explains why, since the government needs cheap money more than anyone.

Big investors are getting a little more careful. The Bank of America survey of fund managers shows cash rising to 3.9% of portfolios, the biggest monthly jump since March, and 48% of them hold fewer bonds than normal, the most since May 2022. A Goldman Sachs gauge of market panic had its biggest 1-day fall in 10 years, and $7 trillion of options expired on 18 September, the 2nd biggest such day ever. On the household side, US median household income hit a record $87,460 in 2025, up 2.6% after inflation, but the poorest 10% saw a 0.8% fall.

India's own money numbers look healthier. Bank credit growth hit a 29-month high in August and deposit growth a 14-year high, so banks are getting deposits to fund their lending rather than squeezing. India's holdings of US Treasury bonds rose to a 10-month high in July, which gives the Reserve Bank of India more dollars to defend the rupee if oil keeps pushing the dollar up.

04

Commodities and trade: who pays and who collects

The oil price is not the whole story. Diesel, metals, steel quotas and exports tell you where the money moves.

First, a check on the price itself. Posts quoted oil at $106. Checked against market data on 18 September, Brent was at $104.34 and US crude at $100.96, down for a 4th session in a row, because Saudi Arabia said it would restore half the East-West pipeline within days and all of it in 6 weeks. Some analysts looking at satellite images say the repair will take months. So the crude price has eased a little, but diesel has not, which means refiners, not oil producers, hold the pricing power right now.

That is why Indian refiners that sell abroad come up. When Europe lost oil supply before, in 2019 (the Abqaiq attack) and 2022 (Russia), the Indian winners were Reliance Industries, MRPL and CPCL. Reliance makes mostly transport fuel and its SEZ refinery is built to export. MRPL exports about 40% of its output and half of what it makes is diesel and jet fuel, exactly what Europe lacks. CPCL gains indirectly, since it sells to its parent Indian Oil at import-parity prices, which rise with world fuel prices. Even so, Reliance shares trade 6.5% below their 200-week average price, which has not happened since 2013, so the market is not yet paying for this.

The war also creates rebuilding work in the Gulf. Engineers India says it is in talks with Saudi Arabia and the UAE on infrastructure that bypasses the Strait of Hormuz. Pipe and project firms like Welspun Corp, Man Industries, Maharashtra Seamless, Ratnamani Metals, VA Tech Wabag (water), Kalpataru Projects and Larsen & Toubro are named as possible gainers. Jefferies kept a Rs 5,000 target on L&T, saying the Middle East and Indian private companies drive its orders, while its 10%-12% growth guidance already allows for delays caused by the war.

Metals are the other big mover. Rating agency ICRA expects Indian demand for non-ferrous metals (aluminium, zinc, copper, lead) to grow 8%-9% in FY27 and industry operating margins to widen by about 4 percentage points to around 35%. The world aluminium market is expected to be 1 million tonnes short in 2026 because of the West Asia war and the closure of the Mozal smelter in Mozambique. Hindustan Zinc keeps margins above 58% with a record-low zinc cost of $851 a tonne, and Hindalco and Nalco gain from high aluminium prices. The cheap input (alumina, down 13%) against a dear output (aluminium, up 25%) is what widens the margin.

Trade deals are shifting who wins too. The EU cut duty-free steel quotas from all countries to 18.35 million tonnes (about 47% lower) and doubled the tariff above quota to 50%. India secured a 1.9 million tonne country quota plus access to a 0.9 million tonne shared pool, which China does not have. But the bigger test is CBAM, the EU's carbon border tax, where the cost could average about 35%, and India is setting up at least 10 local agencies to certify emissions so its mills do not depend on foreign auditors.

Indian exports held up across many goods. Shrimp exports hit a record 81,674 tonnes in July, up 8.2%. Textile exports rose 16.1% to Rs 29,776 crore in August. Construction equipment exports should cross 20,000 units in FY27, up from 11,990 in FY24, now going to the US, Europe and Australia rather than Nepal or Bangladesh. Utility vehicle exports rose 23% while small car exports fell 16%. Separately, a Chinese rare earth company is trying to buy the 7th-largest shareholder of MP Materials, the main US rare earth miner, so the fight over critical minerals is moving into company ownership.

05

India: what the government did, and how the market took it

A big chip push, a new fee on UPI, strong tax numbers and regulators stepping into Tata, HDFC Bank and Nestle.

Semicon 2.0: bigger money, stricter rules

The second phase of India's chip programme, Semicon 2.0, has an incentive budget of about Rs 1.27 trillion (Rs 1.27 lakh crore) across 6 parts: chip design, machines and materials, new fabs (chip factories), assembly and testing (called ATMP or OSAT), research and skills. Minister Ashwini Vaishnaw said it has drawn $11-12 billion of investment pledges, and chip design will get priority, with at least 200 design start-ups targeted against 105 in the first round. Applied Materials committed $5 billion over 10 years, Tata Electronics signed 7 agreements, and Nexperia, the Dutch car-chip maker, picked Tata Electronics to make and package its power chips, Tata's first big named customer. That last one matters because China once used Nexperia's packaging plant in Dongguan as a pressure point, so moving work to India reduces that risk for Nexperia.

The rules are tighter this time. An approved chip plant cannot sell or mortgage any part of the project until the whole project is in commercial production, and it must keep producing for at least 3 years. Land, temporary buildings, technology transfer fees, interest during construction and R&D do not count as capital spending for subsidy. So the scheme pays for real machines and real output, not paper projects. Fujifilm will put about Rs 800 crore into a chemicals plant in Dholera, but a new material takes about 16 months to be approved by a chip customer, so revenue there starts only around FY28. Suchi Semicon began commercial production at its Surat assembly plant, and a first precision manufacturing institute was announced.

UPI's new fee, and the fight over who keeps it

From 15 October, shop payments on UPI above Rs 2,000 will carry a 0.4% merchant fee (MDR), capped at Rs 300. Railways, telecom, insurance, fuel and farm inputs pay a flat Rs 5 instead. Paytm shares rose nearly 2%, but the gain may be smaller than it looks: the 0.4% is shared across banks and apps, and brokers disagree on Paytm's share, as the table in section 08 shows. Petrol pump owners in Mumbai have already said they may stop taking UPI unless fuel is fully exempted.

Regulators step into company fights

At Tata Sons, the question is whether the group's holding company should list on the stock market. Tata Trusts, the majority owner, says it has not agreed to a listing and that the board will look at all options. Tata Trusts chairman Noel Tata presented a plan from the Shapoorji Pallonji group, which owns 18.37% of Tata Sons and needs cash to repay high-cost debt: Tata Sons would buy back its shares for at least Rs 25,000 crore in 2 parts over 18 months through a court-approved capital reduction. Directors Venu Srinivasan and Vijay Singh now favour listing, Noel Tata opposes it, and chairman N Chandrasekaran seems ready to follow whatever the regulator requires.

At HDFC Bank, the RBI will choose the next chief executive from 2 names: Kaizad Bharucha from inside and Anup Bagchi, now head of ICICI Prudential Life. Some shareholders object that the regulator, not the owners, has the final word at a private bank. Food regulator FSSAI started legal action against Nestle India after a follow-up baby formula sample failed a lab test (NAN Excella and Lactogen are the products named). SEBI barred Kore Digital and its top people over fraud allegations and stopped its move to the main NSE board. The Supreme Court allowed NSE to settle its old co-location cases with SEBI, which cleared the way for its IPO.

06

India: where the value really sits

Cases where the easy answer (more capacity, more loans, more orders) and the careful answer are different.

Bearings: the factory is not the same as the profit

Bearing makers have plenty of demand but not always the output to match. At Schaeffler India, sales to carmakers grew 33.3% but the spare-parts business grew only 9.9%, because its Hosur plant served car companies first and left the aftermarket as "second preference", in the words of Harsha Kadam. Timken India's new Bharuch line runs at 40%-45% of capacity and aims for 70%, close to breakeven. SKF India's industrial arm has about Rs 175 crore of new gearbox and tractor orders, but currency and separation costs cut margins by about 3%. So the useful measure is how much of a plant is actually used and how much cash it makes, not how big it is. NRB Bearings is the different case: it won a Sukhoi 30 bearing order that fewer than 5 companies in the world can make, and expects aerospace to bring Rs 300 crore of revenue and Rs 90 crore of profit by FY31.

Small finance banks: safer loans earn less

At Ujjivan Small Finance Bank, secured loans (backed by gold, homes or property) grew 42.7% and are now over 50% of the book; gold loans grew 248.6% and bad microfinance loans fell from 2.68% to 1.72%. But bigger business loans pull the interest earned down from about 11%-11.5% to around 10.5%. Management says lower costs and fewer losses make up for it, and it raised its FY27 return-on-assets goal to 1.8%-2%. AU Small Finance Bank grew gold loans 130% while freezing back-office hiring, and Equitas raised its return goal while cutting its margin goal from 7.24% to 7.1%. So profit now has to come from lower costs, not wider spreads, and the proof will be in how loan batches perform as they age.

Fibre, cables and pipes: orders are the test

For optical fibre makers STL Tech and HFCL, the view is that 3 things matter: new US tariff risk (seen as low now), whether AI spending slows (watch the data, not the headlines), and fresh orders, which matter most. When the US added a 25% tariff on India, STL's margins fell by about 7.6 percentage points, and tariffs once reached 52%, so uncertainty itself delays orders and squeezes prices. Welspun Corp is called the AI-linked name best protected by tariffs, since it also makes pipes inside the US, where the tariff does not apply. Polycab shares fell 7% after UltraTech Cement launched Ultravolt wires with Rs 1,800 crore of investment, but cables, which need 2-4 years of approval for power and industrial buyers, are 70%-75% of Polycab's sales, so the threat is mostly in the simpler wire business.

Ships, trains and defence

Mazagon Dock signed to be the anchor yard for 2 new shipbuilding clusters, at Dighi in Maharashtra and Dugarajapatnam in Andhra Pradesh, each planned at 1.2 million gross tonnes a year; the Andhra yard alone needs about Rs 15,000 crore and could create 45,000 jobs. So a warship builder is moving into large commercial ships, a much lower-margin business. BEML won a Rs 5,400 crore order for bullet train coaches. HAL handed over Tejas twin-seat trainers and HTT-40 trainers, but delays to the LCA Mk1A fighter and a further delay in the AMCA tender remain the complaint. BEL won Rs 648 crore of orders for jammers and AI systems.

07

India consumption: people pay more, but for fewer things

Fees quietly going up, a long festive season, and more money going to the top end of every category.

Prices are rising in small ways that add up. IndiGo raised its infant fee by 50% to Rs 3,000, excess baggage to Rs 800 a kg from Rs 700, priority check-in to Rs 650, and the fee for children flying alone to Rs 5,999. With about 1 million infants flying a year, the infant fee alone could add roughly Rs 100 crore of revenue. Airlines are doing this because fuel is dear and base fares are hard to raise, so the extras carry the load.

The festive season is longer this year. An extra month in the Hindu calendar stretches it from Onam to Chhath to about 80 days, from 52 last year, and Diwali falls on 8 November, 19 days later than last year. Big online sales from Flipkart and Amazon are expected to start around 9-10 October. Datum Intelligence expects festive online sales of Rs 1.5-1.55 lakh crore, up 25%-29%, with quick commerce (10-minute delivery apps) taking 16% against 12% last year. Dark stores (small warehouses serving 2-3 km) grew 34% to 7,153 in more than 450 cities, so apps can stock festival items pin code by pin code. But the average basket is expected to shrink 1.5%, which means growth comes from more buyers and more orders, not bigger bills.

Spending is moving up-market. Titan wants a 25% share of watches priced above Rs 25,000, launching mechanical watches at Rs 48,000-55,000; its watch business grew 21% to Rs 1,543 crore in the June quarter, with analogue watches growing in the mid-20s% and smartwatches shrinking. It still imports hairsprings, a tiny part only a handful of firms in the world make. Timex India has also moved to premium and licensed brands, with FY26 income of about Rs 848 crore and a margin near 14.5%. Apple put the iPhone 18 Pro and Pro Max on sale in India first, before the cheaper models, which helps sellers like Redington and Electronics Mart. Wealth is also spreading beyond the big metros, to cities like Kolhapur, Raipur, Rajkot, Indore and Ludhiana.

Travel is growing too. India's cruise market was worth Rs 830 crore in FY25 and Crisil projects Rs 1,820-2,250 crore by FY31. Cordelia Cruises is adding 2 ships and routes from Kochi to Colombo and Male from October 2026, and Thomas Cook India sees demand for short 3-7 night Asian trips. Jewellery demand from weddings and festivals helps chains like PN Gadgil Jewellers and Lalithaa Jewellery.

2 warnings sit under this. Health insurance claims are a sore point, with complaints that Star Health and Care Health reject or delay claims, and public-sector insurers are rated better on payouts, so buyers are starting to judge insurers on claims, not price. And rooftop solar, a household purchase the government pays part of, fell 11.6% in August: India has 17 gigawatts installed across 5.46 million homes, but the Rs 75,021 crore PM Surya Ghar subsidy scheme ends in March 2027 and the industry wants clarity on a next phase by December 2026.

Story versus numbers

IPO fever, fund performance and AI claims, checked against what the figures actually say.

The IPO rush

6 companies listed on the main board on 17 September, the most on a single day since at least 1995. The hurry is partly because many approvals expire on 30 September. Listing gains were mostly strong, and 81 companies have raised Rs 1.04 trillion in 2026 so far, against Rs 1.76 trillion in all of 2025.

The biggest issue is NSE's own Rs 22,560 crore IPO, which closes on 21 September. It was 43% booked on day 1 (big institutions only 19%) and fully booked on day 2 at 1.04 times, with institutions at 1.3 times and retail at 0.7 times; it had already raised Rs 6,746 crore from about 189 anchor investors, including LIC and Norway's oil fund. Other deals show private money is active: OnEMI Technology (the Kissht lender) raised Rs 832 crore at Rs 314.11 a share only 4 months after an IPO priced at Rs 171; Advent International is paying Rs 3,150 crore for 24.9% of Yatharth Hospital; and JSW One Platforms plans a Rs 3,000 crore IPO.

Do fund managers beat the index?

Indian funds investing abroad through GIFT City mostly lost to their benchmarks in their first year. DSP's fund fell 8% while the world index rose 16.4%, because it avoided AI chip stocks and held about a quarter in cash. PPFAS's active PMS rose 2.1% against 16.8% for the S&P 500, while its own passive S&P 500 fund rose 16.6%. Marcellus returned 3.7% against 22.2%, and Mirae Asset 1.47% against 8.2%. The best, Unifi, returned 15.6% because it owned AI chip makers and cut cash from 25% to 9%. So the funds that sat in cash fell furthest behind.

A second claim says 70% of Indian large-cap funds beat the Nifty 100 over 10 years. Checked against the official S&P SPIVA scorecard to December 2025, the opposite holds: 76.29% of large-cap funds did worse than their benchmark over 10 years. The 2 claims likely use different yardsticks (a price index that leaves out dividends makes funds look better), so ask which index version is being used before trusting either.

AI: bold claims, partial numbers

Anthropic says its Claude model led 26% of its own AI research work in August, up from under 1% in February, but "led" means doing most of a task under human watch, and it is the company's own measure. A rough model shared in the discussion: at $65 billion of yearly revenue and 2-5 gigawatts, Anthropic earns $13-32.5 billion per gigawatt; covering compute alone needs $50-62.5 billion a year per gigawatt used for customers. Kaynes Technology is looking at a $1 billion new chip-assembly project while its first Sanand plant is still ramping up, with only Rs 100 crore of Rs 250-300 crore expected chip revenue booked. Coforge's chief executive Sudhir Singh spoke of an "AI deflationary squeeze", meaning software work is getting cheaper, and TCS pulled IT shares down. Long-term numbers back the caution on slow growers: Dabur profit grew 4% a year for 10 years and its share price 3%; Wipro 4% and 6%.

09

What one region is doing that another is not

China builds and controls, Europe pays for energy, the US borrows, and India picks up the work that moves.

China is doubling down on factories and holding on to its edge. President Xi Jinping called advanced manufacturing the "backbone" of China's economy and asked for supply chains that rely less on others. China is also keeping its best LFP battery technology at home through export controls while pushing sodium-ion batteries in its own market. New exit rules are so loosely written that officials can stop AI and green-tech experts from travelling abroad, which could cost China consulting export work. Huawei said its chairman David Wang will launch 2 new AI chips in 2027, the Ascend 960DT and 960PR, and a Chinese memory maker is moving into flash storage to take on Samsung and SanDisk.

Chinese AI labs are moving fast on cheap, open models. Zhipu AI (Z.ai) says its GLM-5.3 model, working as an "infra agent", helped move its Flash model onto Chinese chips and tripled throughput in under 2 weeks. Many new US products are quietly built on Chinese open-weight models (models whose code anyone can download and run), running on US servers. There is a live argument about whether the US could cut China off by banning older DUV chip-making machines; the counter view is that the Netherlands, home of ASML, gets a say and that China is already building more of these machines itself.

Europe is paying the most. Its economy shrank 6.1% in the pandemic, then faced the Ukraine war energy shock, fell behind in AI (about $50 billion of AI venture money between 2020 and 2025 against about $500 billion in the US), was hit by US tariffs on more than $600 billion of exports, and is now in its worst energy crisis. Volkswagen cut its full-year operating margin forecast to up to 1% from 4%-5.5%, and its shares fell 7.5%. Germany's AfD party is preparing talks with Moscow to restart cheap Russian gas.

On nuclear power, the US is still the world's largest producer, about twice France, while China is building fastest. The point made is that nuclear stalled in the West less because of rules and fear than because nobody could make money building a new plant against cheap shale gas, cheap solar and rising interest rates, so long-term power contracts from tech companies may matter more than faster permits.

Arguments worth keeping open

Real disagreements, with both sides stated and a way to check who is right.

Is AI spending about to overshoot?

This is the richest argument, and it has good evidence on both sides. On the worried side: margin debt is at a record share of GDP, a single buyer spent about $100 million on 2-week bets on Micron, SanDisk, Intel and Marvell rising, token prices (what AI firms charge per unit of text) are at record lows, over 300 US councils are pausing data centres, and Techno Electric in India flags delays on its data centre work. On the confident side: Nvidia's Jensen Huang expects to sell twice as many chips next year, Lam Research's finance chief Douglas Bettinger says the bigger fear is missing demand, not building too much, and a target of 10 million AI chips shipped implies roughly 30 gigawatts of new capacity. A Carlyle paper puts the split well: an engineer sees a sensible effort to remove a real limit, while a statistician sees a curve with no past example. So watch 3 things rather than the mood: new orders at fibre and cable firms, power actually connected (not announced), and whether revenue per gigawatt holds up as token prices fall.

Words explained

Every technical term used above, in 1 plain sentence.

Brent crudeThe main world price for oil, set by oil from the North Sea.
DieselFuel for trucks, trains, ships and farm machines; it is now dearer than crude alone would suggest.
RefineryA plant that turns crude oil into petrol, diesel and jet fuel.
Strait of HormuzA narrow sea channel near Iran through which about a fifth of the world's oil normally passes.
East-West pipelineSaudi Arabia's pipe that carries oil to the Red Sea port of Yanbu, avoiding Hormuz.
Strategic Petroleum ReserveEmergency oil the US government keeps for a supply crisis.
LNGNatural gas cooled into a liquid so it can be shipped.
Gigawatt (GW)1,000 megawatts of power, roughly the output of 1 large nuclear reactor.
BilletA rough steel bar that is later rolled into rods and TMT bars.
Import-parity priceA price set as if the product were imported, so it follows world prices.
Basis point0.01%; 25 basis points is 0.25%.
YieldThe yearly return a lender demands for lending to a government or company for a set time.
Yield spreadThe gap between 2 yields; a wider gap means lenders see more risk in 1 borrower.
Treasury bill / bondShort-term (bill) and long-term (bond) US government borrowing.
Rate checkA central bank calling banks to ask about currency prices, as a warning it may step in.
Yen carry tradeBorrowing cheaply in yen to buy higher-paying assets elsewhere.
Basis tradeHedge funds buying Treasury bonds and selling matching futures, using much borrowed money, to earn a small gap.
Margin debtMoney investors borrow from brokers to buy shares.
Quad witchingA day when 4 kinds of options and futures expire together.
Call optionA contract that pays off if a share rises above a set price by a set date.
Fiscal dominanceWhen government borrowing, not the central bank, drives interest rates and markets.
ICRA / CRISIL / CAREIndian credit rating agencies that grade how safely a company can repay debt.
Operating margin (EBITDA margin)Profit before interest, tax and depreciation, as a share of sales.
EBITDAEarnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation.
Operating cash flow (OCF)Cash a business actually brings in from its normal work.
P/E ratioShare price divided by yearly profit per share.
EV/EBITDACompany value including debt, divided by EBITDA.
ROAReturn on assets: yearly profit as a share of everything a bank or firm owns.
NIMNet interest margin: what a bank earns on loans minus what it pays on deposits, as a share of assets.
Secured loanA loan backed by gold, a home or other property the lender can take.
SlippageLoans that turn bad during a period.
CASACurrent and savings account deposits, a bank's cheapest money.
MDRMerchant discount rate: the fee a shop pays on a digital payment.
P2M UPIUPI payments from a person to a merchant.
STTSecurities transaction tax, charged on share and derivative trades.
F&OFutures and options: contracts on the future price of shares or indices.
IPOInitial public offering: a company's first sale of shares to the public.
Anchor investorA big investor who commits to an IPO before it opens to the public.
QIB / NII / retailIPO quotas for large institutions, rich individuals and small investors.
Preferential allotmentNew shares sold to chosen investors rather than the public.
QIPQualified institutional placement: quick sale of new shares to big institutions.
NCDNon-convertible debenture: a bond that cannot turn into shares.
Capital reductionA court-approved process where a company cancels and pays for some of its shares.
NCLTNational Company Law Tribunal, the court for company matters in India.
PMSPortfolio management service: a managed share portfolio for richer investors.
GIFT CityIndia's international finance centre in Gujarat, where funds can invest abroad.
Passive fund / ETFA fund that copies an index instead of picking shares.
SPIVAS&P's regular scorecard of how active funds do against their index.
Total-return indexAn index that includes dividends, a fairer yardstick for funds.
200-week moving averageThe average share price over about 4 years.
Semicon 2.0The second phase of India's chip-making incentive programme.
FabA factory that makes chips on silicon wafers.
OSAT / ATMPChip assembly, testing and packaging, done after the chip is made.
DUV / EUVOlder (DUV) and newest (EUV) machines that print chip patterns with light.
MOSFETA power-control chip used in cars and electronics.
Post-CMP cleanerA chemical that cleans wafers after polishing in chip making.
Open-weight modelAn AI model whose files anyone can download and run.
TokenA small piece of text; AI firms charge by the number of tokens.
InferenceRunning a trained AI model to answer users.

AluminaThe white powder refined from bauxite and smelted into aluminium.
